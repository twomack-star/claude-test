Bonding two SIMs into one connection

Cradlepoint R1900 (5G) plus CBA1250 (LTE), two different carriers, combined into a single faster connection — and verified.

Hardware R1900 + CBA1250 Steps 13 Time ~3 hrs plus licensing Print this — there are values to write down

Read this before step 1. Two things get confused, and the whole procedure depends on the difference.

Load balancing sends different connections down different links. Ten people browsing get spread across both SIMs. But any single download still rides one SIM, so nothing individual gets faster.

Bonding splits a single connection's packets across both SIMs. That is what makes one download faster than one SIM can manage — and it only works if something out on the internet puts those packets back in order. That something is the NetCloud Service Gateway. The two devices on your bench cannot bond on their own.

So the test that matters is a single-connection speed test. Multi-connection speed tests get faster from plain load balancing and will tell you bonding works when it doesn't.

Before you start

Gather all of this. Missing the last item stops the job at step 8.

  • Cradlepoint R1900 with a SIM installed, powered up
  • Cradlepoint CBA1250 with a SIM installed, powered up
  • Two SIMs on two different carriers — same carrier means one outage takes both links
  • Ethernet cable, CBA1250 to R1900
  • A laptop to test from, and NetCloud Manager login
  • An iperf3 server you can reach, or see step 5 for the alternative
  • SD-WAN / Intelligent Bonding licence on the NetCloud account, and a Service Gateway
Check this first

Confirm the SD-WAN / Intelligent Bonding entitlement is active on the account and applied to this device group. Ask your Cradlepoint rep — don't assume. When it's missing, the overlay still comes up and quietly behaves as load balancing, and you can lose a day to chasing MTU settings that were never the problem.

Part A · Wire it up

Goal: the R1900 sees two working WAN connections.

1

Bring up both modems and record what they're on

  • Confirm both devices show connected in NetCloud Manager
  • Open each device's modem status and note carrier, signal, and band
Why

Once the Ethernet cable is in, the R1900 can't see anything about the CBA1250's cellular side — that WAN just looks like an Ethernet port. If throughput disappoints later, this is the only record of whether the LTE link had decent signal to begin with.

2

Set the CBA1250 to IP passthrough

  • In the CBA1250's settings, switch from router mode to IP passthrough
  • Confirm it hands its carrier address through rather than its own 192.168.x.x
Why

Bonding decides how to split traffic by watching each link's delay and packet loss. Router mode adds a second layer of address translation with its own buffering, which muddies those readings.

3

Cable them together and make the port a WAN

  • CBA1250's LAN port to an Ethernet port on the R1900
  • On the R1900, set that port's role to WAN, not LAN
  • Confirm the R1900 now lists two connected WANs: the internal 5G modem and the Ethernet
  • Confirm the Ethernet WAN shows a carrier address, not 192.168.x.x — if it doesn't, step 2 didn't take

Part B · Make failure detectable

Goal: the R1900 can tell when the CBA1250's cellular side dies. Don't skip this one.

4

Turn on active connection monitoring for both WANs

  • Enable an active probe (ping or DNS) on the Ethernet WAN
  • Enable an active probe on the internal 5G WAN
  • Point each at something out on the internet — not the CBA1250, not the R1900
  • Use a different target for each WAN
Why this is the one people regret skipping

The Ethernet cable stays electrically connected even when the CBA1250's cellular connection drops. Without a probe out to the internet, the R1900 believes that WAN is healthy and keeps feeding it traffic that goes nowhere. Under bonding this is worse than losing a link outright: the gateway sits waiting for packets that will never arrive, and the whole combined connection stalls rather than just getting slower.

Two different targets, because if both WANs probe the same address and that address goes down, both links fail at once.

Prove it works: pull the SIM from the CBA1250 while something is downloading. Traffic should shift to 5G, and NetCloud should mark the Ethernet WAN failed. If it still reads connected, the probe isn't doing its job — fix that before going on.

Part C · Record the before picture

Goal: know each link's speed on its own. These numbers cannot be taken later — once bonding is on, there is no way back to a single link without undoing the build.

5

Speed test the 5G link on its own

  • On the R1900, disable the Ethernet WAN — actually disable it, don't just move it lower in priority
  • Confirm only one WAN is active
  • Run a single-connection speed test and write the number down
iperf3 -c <your-server> -t 60 -P 1      # upload
iperf3 -c <your-server> -t 60 -P 1 -R   # download
No iperf3 server? Read this

Download one large file over HTTPS from a fast host and watch the sustained rate. That is a single connection, which is what we need.

Don't use a browser speed-test site for this number. Those open many connections at once — which is exactly the thing that looks fast under load balancing and hides whether bonding is working. If you run one anyway, keep it in a separate column and don't base the pass/fail on it.

What to expect later

A working bond lands at roughly 80–90% of the sum, not 100%. Wrapping packets for the tunnel and putting them back in order costs something. Don't treat anything under 100% as broken.

Part D · Turn on bonding

Goal: both links joined into one interface, splitting single connections across both.

8

Confirm the licence and stand up the Service Gateway

  • SD-WAN / Intelligent Bonding entitlement active and applied to this device group
  • Service Gateway deployed — Ericsson-hosted, or your own virtual instance
  • Gateway's own connection sized above your step 7 sum, with room spare
  • Gateway reachable from both carriers
Stop here if the licence isn't confirmed

Everything past this point needs it. Without it you will build something that comes up, looks right, and only load balances.

Why the gateway sizing matters

All bonded traffic travels through the gateway. Size it exactly at your sum and the gateway becomes the new speed limit — you'd have moved the bottleneck rather than removed it.

9

Re-enable both WANs and build the overlay

  • Turn both WANs back on; confirm both connected
  • Build the SD-WAN overlay from the R1900 to the Service Gateway
  • Confirm the R1900 shows the overlay up before continuing
10

Create the bonded interface — set it to aggregation

  • Create a bonded WAN interface
  • Add both members: the internal 5G modem and the Ethernet WAN
  • Set the policy to bandwidth aggregation as the default for all traffic
  • Confirm both members show active in the bonded interface
The setting that decides the whole job

There are other policies available. Balancing spreads whole connections and will not make a single download faster. Duplication sends every packet down both links — useful for something that must never drop, but it doubles the data you pay for and makes nothing faster. For one fast connection, you want aggregation.

Not working The single-connection result is about the same as the faster link alone. Both links may well be carrying traffic, but single connections aren't being split. That's load balancing. Go to the table below.

If step 12 failed

Work down the list. It's ordered by how often each one turns out to be the cause.

#CheckWhat you're looking for
1Licence applied to this group Active on the account isn't enough. Not applied here means the overlay comes up and load balances — the commonest cause by a distance.
2Policy is aggregation Balancing is often the default. It will never raise single-connection speed.
3Both members really in the interface Easy to add one and miss the second. Check the WAN device names, not just the count.
4MTU and MSS Symptom is odd: most things work, some transfers stall. If large packets are being dropped the bond can't fill both links.
5One member marked unhealthy Check per-link status at the gateway, not just on the router. An excluded member means you're on one link.
6Router's encrypted throughput rating A router's plain rating is higher than its rating with encryption on. If your step 7 sum is above the R1900's encrypted rating, the router is the ceiling and no setting changes it.
7Your test server A server slower than the two links combined caps every result and looks exactly like a failed bond.

Two things to know before quoting a number

Latency goes up, not down

Bonded traffic detours through the Service Gateway, and packets wait briefly to be put back in order. That's the cost of bonding. Anything that cares more about responsiveness than speed — voice, remote desktop, gaming — may be better off not bonded.

The number moves through the day

The two carriers get busy independently. Re-run steps 11 and 12 at a different time of day before treating any figure as the spec.
